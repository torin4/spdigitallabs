Getting past the intermediate plateau in Japanese

Somewhere around upper-intermediate, roughly B1 to B2 on the CEFR scale, Japanese stops getting visibly better. You can read most things and hold a conversation, and another year of the same habits changes very little. Here is why that happens, and what kinds of practice tend to move it.

Why progress stalls

The words you still need are rarer

The most common words turn up everywhere, so each one you learn pays off the same day. By upper-intermediate, a word you don't know might appear once in an article, or once a month. Learning it changes nothing you can feel. You're still improving, but the improvement is spread so thinly that it's hard to see.

You can get by with what you have

A beginner who lacks a word is stuck. You aren't. You understand 懸念 (けねん) when you read it, but when you speak you say 心配 every time, and everyone understands. Nothing forces the newer word out of you, so it stays a word you recognise and never one you use.

Understanding hides the gaps

Recognising something is easier than recalling it. Reading and listening only ask you to recognise, so they can feel fluent long after your own output has stopped growing. Kanji make this worse, because you can get the meaning of a word from its characters without knowing how it's said. Why you can read a kanji word but not say it goes into that.

Nobody corrects you any more

Once you're easy to understand, people answer what you said and ignore how you said it. Mistakes that don't block communication, a particle here, a slightly wrong register there, can stay for years.

What moves it

Produce, don't only recognise

Pulling an answer out of your memory strengthens it more than looking at the answer again. So make the practice ask for something: type the reading before you see it, say the sentence before you hear it, summarise the article before you reread it. Rereading and picking from a list feel smooth because they ask for less, and they teach less for the same reason.

Read at the right difficulty

Text that's too easy has nothing new in it. Text that's too hard turns into decoding, with a dictionary open for every line. The useful range is text you can mostly follow, with a few words on each page you have to work out or look up.

Vary the kind of text too. News, fiction and business email each have their own vocabulary.

Speak, and find out what was wrong

Speaking practice without feedback mostly repeats what you can already do. You need to say something slightly beyond your comfortable range, and then learn which parts a native speaker would have said differently. How to practise speaking Japanese when you have nobody to practise with covers how to do that alone.

Measure something honest

A count of days studied in a row measures attendance. At this level, better questions are: of the words I met this month, how many can I read aloud correctly? Can I explain an article I just read without looking at it? These go up slowly, but they reflect what you can do.

Doing it in PeraPera

PeraPera is a web app built around these three kinds of practice.

  • Find where words start to get hard. Find my level is a 16-word reading test. You see one word at a time and type its reading, or skip it. It starts at C1. Get a word right and the next comes from the level above; miss or skip it and the next comes from the level below. The result is one of six levels (B1, B2, C1, C1+, C2, C2+), shown with the levels below it marked solid, yours marked frontier and the ones above marked reach. You can retake it any time.
  • Review at that level. The test reschedules the 107 words built into the app. Those above your frontier are due straight away, those at it in two days, and those below it six or fifteen days later. Words that came up in the test are adjusted by how you did on them.
  • Review by producing. On a reading card you see the word in a sentence and type its reading in hiragana before the meaning is shown. The same words also come back with the word blanked out of its sentence, and as listening cards where you hear the word and choose its meaning from four. Reading cards make up half of the rotation.
  • Read at a level you choose. In Read something, you describe what you want to read, then pick one of the six levels and a length: short, medium or long, about 5, 10 or 16 sentences. The level starts at your placement result. Tap a word for its reading and meaning, and add it to your review from there. Furigana and the English translation stay hidden until you switch them on. Two or three comprehension questions follow the passage.
  • Speak with feedback. Conversation practice builds a short role-play around the review words you get wrong most, and your partner adds a short note when something you said was unnatural. The speaking test is an interview that ends with a written report.
  • One number. The home screen shows your reading accuracy: readings typed correctly out of all those you've been asked for. There are no streaks.

The level names follow the CEFR scale, but they're the app's own rough bands, worked out from which words you could read. They aren't a certificate.

The passages, the conversation and the reports are written by AI. AI can get things wrong, including about Japanese, so check anything that looks odd against a dictionary or a native speaker.
