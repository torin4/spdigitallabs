What a clipboard manager is, and who needs one on a Mac

14 November 2026 · 5 min read

Mac app · Coming soon

Tuck

A clipboard manager for Mac that stays out of sight. It remembers the text, files, images and screenshots you copy, and nothing leaves your Mac.

See Tuck

The Mac clipboard holds one thing. Copy something new and whatever was there before is gone. A clipboard manager keeps a list of what you copied, so you can get an earlier item back.

What the Mac clipboard keeps

When you press Cmd+C, macOS puts your selection on the clipboard. Cmd+V pastes it, as many times as you like, until you copy something else. To see what's on it right now, switch to the Finder and choose Edit > Show Clipboard.

That's all it does. In particular:

  • It has one slot. The second thing you copy replaces the first. There's no way to ask for the one before.
  • It doesn't last. Whatever is on the clipboard is lost when you restart your Mac.
  • You can't search it. There's only one item, so there's nothing to search.

Universal Clipboard lets you copy on an iPhone and paste on a Mac signed in to the same Apple Account, but it's still one item. macOS 26 added a basic clipboard history to Spotlight. It's off until you turn it on, and it keeps items for a limited time before dropping them. On earlier versions of macOS there is no history at all.

What a history adds

You can get back what you overwrote

The common accident is copying an address, then copying something else on the way to pasting it. With a history, the address is one line down the list. You don't have to go back and find it again.

You can copy first and paste afterwards

Moving five things from one document to another normally means ten trips: copy, switch, paste, switch back. With a history you copy all five, switch once, and paste them one after another.

You can find things by typing

A list of a thousand clips is only useful if you can find one. A clipboard manager lets you type a few letters and narrows the list to the clips that contain them: the tracking number from this morning, the command you ran last week.

You can keep the things you reuse

Some text gets pasted again and again: your address, a standard reply, a long command. Most clipboard managers let you mark those to keep, so they don't drop off the end of the history.

Who it suits

  • People who move text between apps all day. Support replies, data entry, filling in forms from a spreadsheet, quoting sources into a document.
  • Developers. Commands, error messages, IDs and snippets get copied constantly, and the one you need is often three copies back.
  • People who take a lot of screenshots. A history that keeps images can replace the pile of files on the Desktop. How to copy Mac screenshots to the clipboard covers that.
  • Anyone who has lost a copied paragraph by copying something else before pasting it.

Who it suits less

  • People who copy and paste straight away. If you rarely need anything but the last thing you copied, one slot is enough.
  • People on macOS 26 or later who only need the last few hours. Try the history in Spotlight first. It may be all you need.
  • People who share one Mac user account with someone else. A history is a record of what you copied, and anyone using that account can open it.
  • People who mostly copy sensitive things. Passwords, keys and other people's personal details are exactly what you don't want kept. A good clipboard manager skips much of this, but none can catch everything. Is clipboard history safe? goes through what to check.

How Tuck does it

Tuck is a clipboard manager for Mac from SP Digital Labs. It isn't released yet; it's coming to the Mac App Store and will need macOS 13 or later. This is how it works.

Tuck records the text, files and images you copy while it's running. Nothing appears on screen until you press Cmd+Shift+V, apart from an icon in the menu bar that you can hide in Settings > General. The panel lists your clips newest first, with the selected one shown in full underneath. Type to narrow the list: every word you type must appear in the clip. Press Return and the clip is pasted into the app you were in.

A few details that matter in daily use:

  • Search terms. Add app:safari to show clips copied in one app, or is:text, is:file, is:image or is:pinned to show one kind. Images are found by the text in them.
  • Pasting several at once. Mark clips with Tab, then press Return. They're pasted as one block, in the order you marked them, with a blank line between each.
  • Pins and templates. Pinned clips are never dropped. A pinned clip containing {clipboard} is a template: pasting it puts whatever text is on the clipboard at that spot.
  • Clean pasting. By default, Return removes terminal color codes, trailing spaces and indentation shared by every line. Option+Return pastes the clip untouched, and you can turn the cleaning off in Settings > Panel.
  • No duplicates. Copy the same thing twice and Tuck keeps one entry and moves it to the top.

Limits worth knowing

  • The history has a size. Tuck keeps the newest 1,000 clips and 200 images unless you change the numbers in Settings > History. Older ones are dropped, except pins.
  • Very large copies are skipped. Text over 1 MB and images over 60 MB are not recorded.
  • Files are recorded by location. When you copy a file in the Finder, Tuck keeps the path to it, not a copy of the file.
  • It only records while it's running. Turn on Launch at login in Settings > General if you want it there every day.
  • Pasting for you needs permission. Tuck needs Accessibility access to press Cmd+V in the app you were in. Without it, the clip you pick is placed on the clipboard and you paste it yourself.
  • The shortcut may already be taken. Some apps use Cmd+Shift+V for pasting without formatting. If you rely on that, choose a different shortcut in Settings > General.
  • There is no sync. Tuck has no account and never connects to the internet, so the history exists on one Mac only.
