How to keep a workout log you will actually use

14 November 2026 · 5 min read

Web app

Lift

A workout log that takes one tap per set. Before every exercise it suggests a weight from your own history and shows you why.

See Lift

A workout log is only useful if it answers a question next time: what did I do here last week, and what should I do today? Most logs fail because they record too much to keep up, or too little to answer that.

What to write down

For each set, two numbers: the weight and the reps. For each exercise, its name and the order you did it in. For the session, the date. Everything else in this post is optional, and those few things are not.

Write each set down when you finish it, not at the end of the session. By the time you are changing your shoes, you won't remember whether the third set of rows was 9 reps or 10, and that difference is exactly what decides whether to add weight next time.

One line per set is better than a summary. "3 × 10 at 40 kg" hides the session where the sets went 10, 10, 7. Written out, a single exercise looks like this:

Next to it, one line for the setup: seat 4, thigh pad 2.

Machine settings

The seat height, the pad position, which pin hole, how wide a grip. These are the things you forget between sessions, and they change the exercise. A leg press with the seat one notch further back is a different lift at the same weight, so last week's number stops being a fair comparison.

Write the setting down once, next to the exercise, and update it only when it changes. It also saves the first minute at each machine.

Skipped sets

If you planned four sets and did three, write that the fourth was skipped. Don't leave a gap, and don't write a zero.

The difference matters when you read the log back. A blank could mean you forgot to write it down. A zero looks like a set you tried and failed. "Skipped" says what happened: the machine was taken, you ran out of time, or you chose to stop. It also keeps the record honest about how much you did that day.

A short note

One line is enough, and only when there is something to say: the machine was a different model, you trained after a long gap, the last set felt easier than the reps suggest. Notes explain numbers that would otherwise look odd a month later.

What to leave out

Anything you won't keep up. Tempo, time under tension and a rating for every set are all fine if you use them, but a log with five fields per set tends to be abandoned by the third week. Start with weight and reps. Add a field only when you have wanted it more than once.

Reading it back

A log earns its keep in the minute before each exercise. Three ways to read it:

  • Last time. Look at the same exercise in the previous session, set by set. That tells you the weight to start from and the reps to match or pass.
  • One exercise over weeks. Follow a single exercise back a month or two. A weight that hasn't moved is easier to see in a row of sessions than from memory.
  • The week as a whole. Count the sets you did for each part of the body. How to count weekly sets per muscle group shows how.

Paper does all of this. A notebook with one page per session works, and so does the record sheet some gyms print. The weak point of paper is reading back: finding the last time you did an exercise means turning pages.

None of this is medical advice or coaching. A log records what you did. It doesn't tell you what is right for your body.

Doing it in Lift

Lift is a workout log built around these points. You build a routine once, with the exercises in the order you do them, and each workout is a run through that list.

  • One tap per set. Once you have done an exercise before, each set opens with a suggested weight filled in and a Last time column showing the weight and reps of the same set in your previous session. Tap the check and the set is logged at that weight and the exercise's rep target, and the rest timer starts. Change a number first only if it was different.
  • Skipped is its own state. Tap a set number to mark the set skipped, or Skip to skip the whole exercise. Any set you haven't logged when you finish is saved as skipped. Skipped sets are left out of set counts and volume.
  • Setup carries over. Each exercise has a Setup field for the seat or pad position. Whatever you last wrote there is filled in the next time you do that exercise.
  • Notes. Each exercise has a notes box, saved with the session.
  • Rest timer. It counts down the rest time set for that exercise, with buttons to add or take off 15 seconds, or to skip it.
  • Nothing lost mid-workout. If the app is closed during a workout, it reopens on the exercise you were on, with your sets and the timers as they were.

To read the log back, History charts the working weight of any exercise session by session, with the sets of recent sessions listed underneath, and lists every session you have logged. Open a session to see each set, the setup and the notes, and use Edit this session to correct it. "Log a past workout" adds one you forgot, under the date it happened. Body measurements, such as your bodyweight, are kept separately on the Body screen.

Accounts, offline use and your data

Lift works without an account. Your log is then stored in your browser, on that device only, so clearing the browser's data for the site or losing the device loses the log. After the first visit it opens and saves with no connection.

If you sign in with Google, your log is also backed up and kept in step on every device where you sign in. Anything you log offline is synced when you are back online. Signing in is free.

Either way, Settings > Export everything saves your routines, sessions, body measurements and settings as one file, and Import a backup puts a saved file back, replacing what is in the app.

Logging, history and sync are all free. Lift Pro is an optional paid plan that builds routines for you. It doesn't add anything to the log itself.

Who it suits less

  • People who think in pounds. Lift works in kilograms only.
  • People who log a rating for each set. There is no field for how hard a set felt. The notes box is the only place for it.
  • People who train by rep ranges or mark warm-up sets. Each exercise has one rep target, and every set is the same kind of set.
  • People who do something different every session. Lift is quickest when you repeat a routine. You can add an exercise during a workout, but there is no blank session to fill in as you go.
