How to share a house plan with your builder and keep track of versions

14 November 2026 · 5 min read

Web app

Hanjo

A house planner for people building in Japan. Draw on the 910 mm module, see each room in jo and tsubo, then look around the house in 3D.

See Hanjo

A house plan goes through many rounds between you, your family and your builder. Most of the confusion comes from two things: comments scattered across emails and chats, and people looking at different versions of the drawing.

Keep one current plan

Decide where the current plan lives and who is allowed to change it. Everything else is a copy. If you email a PDF, that PDF is out of date the next time you move a wall, and a comment on it may be about something you've already fixed.

If you can, share a link to the plan itself, so whoever opens it sees it as it stands today. When you have to send a file, put the version name and the date on it.

Name each version when you send it

Save a version every time you send the plan to someone or agree something with them, and give it a name that says what changed: "Rev B, bigger kitchen" is more use in three weeks than "final 2". Then a builder who says "the stair in the plan you sent on the 3rd" is pointing at something you can open.

Try big changes on a copy. If you want to see what happens when the bathroom moves upstairs, do it on a separate copy and compare it with the current plan, so a bad idea is easy to throw away.

Put comments on the plan

"The door by the kitchen" can mean three different doors. A comment pinned to a spot on the drawing can only mean one. Whatever tool you use, try to get comments attached to the place they're about, one issue per comment.

Close comments as you deal with them. A list of twenty comments where fifteen are already dealt with hides the five that matter.

Decide who needs what

  • Your partner or family need to look around and say what they think.
  • Your builder or architect need to see exact dimensions and tell you what won't work. They will usually redraw the plan in their own CAD software, so they also need something they can print or measure from.
  • Parents, friends, the bank may only need to look.

Give each person the least access that does the job. And remember that a plan shows the size, shape and orientation of your plot, so think about who gets a link that anyone can open.

Doing it in Hanjo

Sharing in Hanjo is free. Open your design, choose Share and then Start sharing. Hanjo keeps a copy of the current design online and updates it a moment after each change you make. Nobody you share with can change your design. They can look, and some can comment.

People you invite

Under People, add someone's email address and choose a role:

  • Can comment lets them pin comments to the plan and reply.
  • Architect does the same, and can also resolve or reopen other people's comments.
  • View only lets them see the design and read the comments, but not write any.

Hanjo doesn't email the invitation. Copy the invitation link from the dialog and send it yourself. They open it and sign in, with Google or an email address and password, using the address you added. An account is free.

View-only links

A view-only link opens the design for anyone who has it, without signing in. They can't see or write comments. You choose whether it lasts 7 days, 30 days or has no end date. The link is shown once when you create it, so copy it then. You can turn a link off at any time.

What they see

Guests get the plan, the 3D view with its walk-through and sunlight controls, and the areas, checks and rough cost panels. They can also use Print / PDF. They don't see your saved versions, your proposals or any drawings you imported to trace. Those aren't part of the shared copy.

Comments

Once a design is shared, you and anyone who can comment get a Comment tool. Click the plan where the comment belongs and write it. Each comment gets a numbered pin, others can reply, and Resolve hides it from the list until you choose Show resolved. Comments show the email address of whoever wrote them.

Versions and proposals

The Versions tab has Save version, which stores the design as it is under a name you give it. Click a saved version to look at it: Hanjo highlights the walls that differ from the current design, and Restore this version brings it back. New proposal makes a copy to try a change on. You can compare it with the current design, then Accept it or Reject it.

Sending a drawing

Print / PDF opens your browser's print dialog with one A3 landscape sheet per floor, with dimensions, an area table and a title block showing the scale and date. Hanjo picks the scale from 1:50, 1:100, 1:200 or 1:500 so the plot fits. Choose Save as PDF there to get a file, and print at 100% for the scale to be true.

When the builder sends their own drawing back, you can import it and trace or compare it. How to read a Japanese floor plan explains how, along with the units builders use.

Stopping

Stop sharing deletes the online copy, its comments and every link. The design in your account is kept.

Where it falls short

  • Only you can edit. Even the Architect role can't change the design, and two people can't work on it at once.
  • No CAD export. Hanjo prints to PDF and saves a copy as a file that only Hanjo opens. It doesn't export DXF or other CAD formats, so a builder will redraw from the PDF.
  • Comments aren't live. New comments appear when you come back to the window, or within about 30 seconds. Hanjo doesn't send an email when someone comments.
  • Sharing is managed from one browser. The Share controls for a design are in the browser where you started sharing it.
  • Versions aren't shared. Guests always see the current design, so tell them which version name it matches when you ask for comments.

A builder's comments are the start of a professional check, and they don't replace one. Hanjo is a planning tool, not an architect. Have a licensed architect (建築士) or builder check any design before you rely on it. Before you send the plan, it's worth running through How to check a house plan before you commit to it yourself.
