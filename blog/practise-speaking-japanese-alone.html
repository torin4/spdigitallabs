How to practise speaking Japanese when you have nobody to practise with

Speaking is the hardest skill to practise alone, because it seems to need another person. What it needs is two things: a reason to produce Japanese out loud, and a way to find out what was wrong with it. You can arrange both by yourself.

Why speaking falls behind

You can read or listen at any hour, for as long as you like. Speaking only happens when someone is there, so it gets a fraction of the practice time.

Living in Japan doesn't fix this on its own. Daily conversations repeat: the same exchanges at work, in shops and with the same few people. You get quick at those and stay slow at everything else. And once people can understand you, they stop correcting you.

Ways to speak without a partner

Shadowing

Play a recording and say it along with the speaker, a beat behind. It's good for rhythm, speed and getting your mouth used to long phrases. It doesn't practise the harder part, which is building the sentence yourself.

Retelling

Read an article or watch a short video, then explain it out loud in a minute without looking. Notice where you stall or swap in an easier word, because those are the gaps worth filling.

Rehearsing real situations

Pick something you'll actually have to do: a phone call to the ward office, explaining a delay to a client, turning down an invitation. Say your side of it aloud, once in polite speech and once in casual speech. Register is a common weak point at this level, and you can practise switching it alone.

Recording yourself

Record two minutes of any of the above and listen the next day. You'll hear the hesitations, the fillers and the three phrases you lean on for everything.

Getting feedback on what you said

Producing speech alone is the easy half. Finding out what was wrong with it is harder.

  • Listen back. This catches slips: things you know and got wrong at speed. It can't catch mistakes you don't know are mistakes.
  • Write it down. Transcribe exactly what you said and look at it as text. Particle and conjugation errors are easier to see on the page.
  • Ask a person a narrow question. A tutor or a patient friend will give you more if you ask "which three sentences sounded unnatural?" than if you ask them to correct everything.
  • Ask an AI. It will comment on every sentence, at any hour. It's useful for grammar, word choice and register, and it can also be wrong.

Whatever the source, good feedback is about a sentence you actually said, gives you the natural version of it, and is short enough to act on. Then use it: say the corrected sentence aloud, and try to say the same kind of thing again a few days later.

Doing it in PeraPera

PeraPera has four speaking tools. In the first three you hold the mic button and speak, and your browser's speech recognition turns it into text that you can fix before sending. In a browser without speech recognition, you type instead. Your partner's lines are spoken aloud. You can set the voice, the speed and, outside the test, how formal your partner is.

Conversation practice

You choose up to ten words from your review; by default it picks the six you get wrong most. The app writes a short role-play scene that needs those words and tells you who you are and what you're trying to do. After each thing you say, your partner replies and, when something was off, adds one short note in English on naturalness, register or grammar. Each target word is ticked when you use it correctly, and those words are then scheduled further out in your review.

Speaking test

An interview of 6 to 12 questions, which get harder when you answer well and easier when you struggle. Questions are spoken only. You can reveal the Japanese text or an English translation, but each question you needed them for counts against your level. Afterwards you get an estimated speaking level on the app's six-level scale from B1 to C2+, and a written report: strengths, gaps, grammar to review with your sentence and a corrected one, words to learn, and what to practise next.

Guided practice

You pick one weakness, either from your last speaking test or one you type in. You get a short lesson, four spoken drills with one retry each, then a short scene built so that the point has to come up. Each drill answer is marked, with a more natural version when there is one.

Record a conversation

For a real conversation with a real person. You read one line alone at the start so the app knows which voice is yours, then talk for up to ten minutes. The recording is transcribed and split into turns, and you tap any line that was given to the wrong speaker. Only your lines are judged. The report lists what you did well and the lines worth fixing, each with a natural version and a reason. Weaknesses it finds are added to Guided practice.

All of this feedback is written by AI, and AI can get things wrong. Treat a correction as something to check, especially when it contradicts what you hear native speakers say. The feedback is also based on the text of what you said. Nothing in PeraPera assesses your pronunciation or pitch accent.

Who it suits less

  • Beginners. The easiest level is upper-intermediate.
  • People who want pronunciation coaching. You'll need a teacher or a shadowing routine for that.
  • People who want a Japanese interface. The app's screens and the coaching notes are in English.
