Why you can read a kanji word but not say it

You see 相殺 in a report and know at once that it means offsetting. Then you have to say it in a meeting, and you aren't sure whether it's そうさい or そうさつ. Knowing what a kanji word means and knowing how it's read are separate pieces of knowledge, and most reading practice only builds the first.

Two kinds of knowing

Kanji carry meaning. If you know the characters, you can often understand a word you have never heard, which is a real advantage of the writing system. It also means silent reading never checks whether you know the sound.

When the sound is needed, something usually supplies it: furigana, a pop-up dictionary, a subtitle. You look, you nod, and you move on. You recognised the reading. You didn't have to produce it, and recognising is the easier of the two.

Where the gap shows

  • Speaking. You avoid the word, or say it quietly and hope.
  • Typing. Japanese input goes through the reading. If you believe 融通 is ゆうつう, the word won't come up.
  • Listening. A word you'd know on paper goes past unrecognised when someone says it, because the sound was never attached to it.
  • Reading aloud. A document you understood perfectly well turns into a string of hesitations.

Why readings go wrong

Words read as a whole

In 田舎 (いなか), 老舗 (しにせ) and 為替 (かわせ), the reading belongs to the whole word. Nothing about the individual characters will get you there. You know these or you don't.

Sound changes

A character you know well is read slightly differently inside a particular word. 発 is usually はつ, but 発端 is ほったん. 通 is usually つう, but 融通 is ゆうずう.

A reasonable guess that happens to be wrong

Many characters share a part that hints at the sound: 清, 晴, 精 and 静 can all be read せい, like the 青 inside them. Guessing from the familiar part works often enough to become a habit, and the exceptions are where it catches you.

One character, several readings

覆す is くつがえす, 覆う is おおう, and 覆面 is ふくめん. Knowing the character doesn't tell you which reading a given word takes.

How to practise readings so they stick

Produce the reading before you check it

Trying to recall something, and then seeing the answer, fixes it in memory better than reading the answer again. So cover the furigana and commit to a reading first. Typing it in kana is stricter than saying it in your head, because you can't be vague about a long vowel or a small っ.

Learn the word in a sentence

The reading belongs to the word, not to the character, so practise it attached to a word and a context you could meet again. 融通が利かない is easier to keep than 融通 alone.

Spread it out

Coming back to a word tomorrow, then next week, does more than repeating it ten times tonight. A reading you can still produce after a gap is one you actually have.

Keep the ones you got wrong

A confident wrong guess that gets corrected tends to stay corrected. The words worth your time are the ones you misread, so keep a list of them and go back to it.

Attach the sound

Say the word aloud when you check it, and hear it if you can. The aim is to know the word when it's spoken as well as when it's written.
