How to count weekly sets per muscle group

Counting the sets you do for each muscle group in a week is a simple way to see whether your training covers what you think it covers. It takes a log and a few minutes, and it often turns up a part of the body you had quietly stopped training.

What a weekly set target is

A weekly set target is a range, such as 6 to 14 sets a week for chest. You count the sets you did for that muscle group over seven days and see whether the total lands inside the range.

Sets are a convenient unit because they are easy to count and hard to distort. A total of kilograms lifted, the other figure people track, swings with the exercise you chose: a leg press moves far more weight than a lateral raise, and a pull-up moves none on paper. A set is a set on any equipment.

What range to use varies a lot between programs and people. A common starting point is around ten sets a week for a large muscle group and fewer for small ones, adjusted over time to what you can do and recover from. Treat any range as a place to start. It is not a prescription.

Which sets to count

The usual convention is to count working sets, the ones done at your training weight with real effort, and to leave out warm-ups. Sets you planned but didn't do don't count either. Whatever you decide, count the same way every week, or the totals can't be compared.

How to count compound lifts

A bench press works the chest, the shoulders and the triceps. So which gets the set? There are two common answers:

  • One muscle group per exercise. Each exercise is filed under the muscle it mainly trains, and each set counts once. Bench press is chest, a row is back, a squat is quads.
  • Part credit. The main muscle gets a full set and the helpers get a fraction, often a half. Three sets of bench press become 3 for chest and 1.5 each for shoulders and triceps.

The first is simpler and easy to do by hand, but it under-counts the muscles that assist. If your week is mostly presses and rows, your arms will look less trained than they are. The second is closer to what happened but needs a table of every exercise and takes longer. Either works if you stick to it and know which way it leans.

A worked week

Three sessions, counted with one muscle group per exercise:

  • Monday: barbell bench press, lat pulldown, back squat, overhead press. 3 sets each.
  • Wednesday: chest press machine, machine row, leg press, lying leg curl. 3 sets each.
  • Friday: barbell bench press, barbell row, Romanian deadlift, 3 sets each. Barbell curl, 3 sets planned, the last one skipped.

Reading the count

  • Look for zeros first. A muscle group with no sets for several weeks running is the clearest finding. In the example, abs and obliques have nothing, and no exercise in the week is aimed at them.
  • Then look for lopsided pairs. Chest far ahead of back, or quads far ahead of hamstrings, shows up as soon as the numbers are side by side.
  • Read "under" with the convention in mind. Shoulders has 3 sets of its own here, but two bench sessions also involved them. A low count for an assisting muscle means less than a low count for one nothing else trains.
  • Read "over" as a question. A total above your range isn't wrong. It is a prompt to check whether that was the plan.

The fix is usually small: swap one exercise, or add two or three sets where the gap is. Rebuilding the whole week over one count is rarely needed. Look again after a few weeks.

None of this is medical advice or coaching. The count describes what you did. How much is right for you depends on things a tally can't see.

How Lift does it

Lift keeps this count for you on its Stats screen, from the sessions you log.

  • One muscle group per exercise. Every exercise in Lift's library belongs to one of 15 groups, and each set counts once, towards that group. Barbell bench press counts for chest, back squat for quads, deadlift for back, Romanian deadlift for hamstrings, overhead press for shoulders. There is no part credit, so the counts in the table above are the ones Lift would show for that week, against its default ranges. When you create your own exercise, you choose its group.
  • A rolling seven days. The count covers sessions dated today and the six days before, so it moves forward every day. It doesn't reset on Monday.
  • Every logged set counts. Skipped sets are left out. Lift doesn't know how hard a set was and has no warm-up set type, so a warm-up you log is counted like any other set. If you want the count to mean working sets, log only those.
  • A range for each group. Each bar is marked Under, In range or Over against a target range. The ranges start from defaults, for example 6 to 14 for chest, back, quads and shoulders, and 4 to 12 for hamstrings, glutes, abs, biceps, triceps and calves. They are starting points. Tap Edit targets to set your own.
  • Zeros are shown for seven groups. Chest, back, quads, hamstrings, glutes, abs and obliques always appear, even with no sets. The other groups, including shoulders, biceps, triceps and calves, appear only once they have at least one set in the seven days. If one of those is missing from the screen, its count is zero.

The same screen shows the number of sessions and sets in the last seven days, and how the working weight of your recent exercises changed between the last two sessions.
