How to read a Japanese floor plan: jo, tsubo, LDK and the 910 mm grid

14 November 2026 · 5 min read

A Japanese house plan uses a few units and abbreviations you won't find on plans elsewhere. Once you know what a jo, a tsubo and "3LDK" stand for, and why so many dimensions are multiples of 910 mm, the plan reads like any other.

Jo: room sizes counted in tatami mats

One jo (畳) is the area of one tatami mat. Rooms are described by how many mats would cover the floor, whether or not the room has tatami: a 6-jo bedroom, a 16-jo living room.

The catch is that mats are not one size. They vary by region and by type of building. These are the usual nominal sizes:

For property advertising, Japan's fair competition rules for real estate say one jo must be at least 1.62 m². That is the figure most conversions use:

6 jo × 1.62 = 9.72 m²

Treat a jo count as a rough size. Two "6-jo" rooms on different plans can differ by a noticeable amount, so check the dimensions too.

Tsubo: land, floor area and price

One tsubo (坪) is about 3.31 m², roughly the area of two tatami mats. Plots of land, the total floor area of a house and building costs are all commonly quoted in tsubo. A 100 m² house is 100 ÷ 3.3058 = 30.25 tsubo.

Builders often quote a price per tsubo (tsubo-tanka). What that price includes differs from builder to builder, so when you compare quotes, check that they cover the same work.

The 910 mm grid

Traditional timber houses are laid out in ken, a length of about 1,818 mm that is usually drawn as 1,820 mm. Half of that, 910 mm, is the module most Japanese house plans are drawn on. Walls, posts, doors and windows fall on the grid, which is why you keep seeing 910, 1,820, 2,730 and 3,640 on a plan. Some builders use a 1,000 mm metre module instead.

Dimensions are normally measured to the centre of each wall (壁芯, kabeshin), so the clear space is smaller than the number on the plan. A corridor one module wide is 910 mm from wall centre to wall centre and about 780 mm between the finished walls, depending on how thick the walls are.

The same goes for areas. A typical 6-jo room is 2,730 × 3,640 mm on the grid, which is 9.94 m² to the wall centres. The floor you can actually stand on is a little less.

LDK: counting the rooms

L is living, D is dining and K is kitchen. The number in front counts the other rooms, usually bedrooms. So a 3LDK has three rooms plus a combined living, dining and kitchen space. A 1K is one room with a separate kitchen, and a 1R is a single room.

An S, as in 2SLDK, is a service room: a room that can't be counted as a habitable room, usually because it doesn't have enough window. It's often used as a storeroom or study.

A few other words turn up on most plans:

  • Washitsu is a Japanese-style room, usually with tatami.
  • Genkan is the entrance, where shoes come off.
  • Oshiire is a deep closet, made for futons.
  • Engawa is a strip of floor along the outside edge of the house, like a veranda.
  • Doma is an indoor floor at ground level that you can walk on in shoes.

Coverage and floor area ratio

Two ratios limit how much house a plot can hold. The building coverage ratio (建蔽率, kenpei-ritsu) is the building's footprint divided by the site area. The floor area ratio (容積率, yoseki-ritsu) is the total floor area of all storeys divided by the site area. The limits depend on the plot's use zone and on local rules, and the land listing or the municipality will tell you what they are. On a 150 m² plot with limits of 50% and 100%:

150 m² × 50% = 75 m² footprint, and 150 m² × 100% = 150 m² of floor

Doing it in Hanjo

Hanjo is a house design app built around these conventions. It runs in a browser, you need a free account to use it, and drawing is free.

  • The grid. A new design uses the 910 mm module, and points snap to the half module, 455 mm. You can change the Module setting to 1000. Hold Alt while drawing to place something freely, to the millimetre. Lengths are always in millimetres.
  • Rooms. With the Room tool, drag a rectangle and the walls follow. A room that shares an edge with another reuses the wall that's already there. The Wall tool draws one wall at a time.
  • Jo, tsubo and square metres together. Once you're zoomed in far enough, each room's label shows its area in all three. Hanjo uses 1.62 m² per jo and 3.3058 m² per tsubo, and measures to wall centres.
  • Japanese room types. A room's Use can be LDK, Washitsu, Genkan, Oshiire, Engawa or Doma, among others, and door styles include Fusuma and Shoji.
  • The two ratios. The Checks tab lists each floor's area and the total in square metres and tsubo, and shows coverage and floor area ratio against the limits you enter for your plot.

Hanjo doesn't count your rooms and label the house "3LDK" for you. Its ratios are also simplified: for example, it doesn't apply the floor-area exemptions that garages can get.

Tracing a plan you were given

If a builder or an estate agent has given you a plan, you can draw over it. Use Import a plan to trace, below the tools, and choose a PNG, JPEG or WebP image, or a PDF. Only the first page of a PDF is used. The drawing appears half transparent on the floor you're working on.

Then set its scale. Choose Calibrate scale, click two points on the drawing whose real distance you know, such as the two ends of a dimensioned wall, and type that distance in millimetres. You can drag the drawing into place, change its Opacity and Rotation, and Lock it so it stays put while you draw rooms on top. If the plan was drawn on a 910 mm module, its walls should line up with the grid.

Hanjo is a planning tool, not an architect. Have a licensed architect (建築士) or builder check any design before you rely on it. Once your plan is drawn, How to check a house plan before you commit to it covers what to look for.
