When to add weight to a lift: a simple rule

14 November 2026 · 5 min read

Most people who train with weights ask the same question every session: is today the day to go heavier? A simple rule, written down and followed, answers it before you reach the machine.

The rule

A common approach has three parts:

  • Pick a rep target for the exercise, for example 3 sets of 10.
  • When every set reaches the target at the same weight, add one step next session.
  • When any set falls short, keep the weight and try again next session.

Nothing more is needed to get started. The rule is useful because it settles the decision in advance, from what you actually did last time, so how you feel on the day doesn't come into it.

What counts as a step

A step is the smallest increase the equipment allows. On a machine it is one plate of the weight stack, often 2.5 kg, though stacks differ. On a barbell it is the smallest pair of plates the gym has. Dumbbells go up by whatever the rack offers.

Use the smallest step you can. Going from 40 kg to 42.5 kg is an increase of about 6%, which is already a lot to ask for the same ten reps. Skipping a step to save time usually costs you the next two sessions.

A worked example

Here is a chest press, 3 sets with a target of 10 reps, on a machine with 2.5 kg steps:

645 kg10, 10, 845 kg
745 kg10, 9, 940 kg

Session 1 reached the target on every set, so the weight went up. Sessions 2 and 3 fell short on the last set, so the weight stayed, and the last set crept from 8 reps to 9 to 10. That took three sessions at 42.5 kg, which is normal. The reps are where the progress shows between increases.

Sessions 5 to 7 are the other case. Three sessions in a row at 45 kg, and the sets still aren't all reaching 10. The last row, where the weight drops, is covered below.

When you stop moving

Holding a weight for a session or two is part of the rule. Holding it for many sessions with no change is a sign that the rule needs a second half. A common answer is to take the weight down by about a tenth, work back up through weights you can complete, and meet the stuck weight again a few weeks later.

In the example, that is:

45 kg × 0.9 = 40.5 kg, rounded to the 2.5 kg step = 40 kg

How many stuck sessions to allow before easing off is a judgment call. Three is a reasonable place to start.

The version with a rep range

You'll also see this rule written with a range instead of a single target, often called double progression: work between, say, 8 and 12 reps, add reps until every set reaches 12, then add weight and start again from 8. It is the same idea with a wider gap between increases, and it suits equipment with large steps, where the next weight up is a big jump.

A single target is simpler to log and to check, since a set either reached the number or it didn't. If the steps on your equipment are small, it is usually enough.

What the rule doesn't know

  • How the reps were done. Ten reps cut short or bounced are not the ten reps you did last month. Count a rep only if you'd have counted it then.
  • How you are today. A bad night's sleep or a long gap since the last session doesn't appear in the numbers. The rule gives you a starting point, and you can always choose less.
  • Your body. It is arithmetic on a log. It can't tell an easy set from a hard one, and it knows nothing about an old injury.

None of this is medical advice or coaching. If something hurts, stop, and if you have a health concern, talk to a doctor before changing how you train.

How Lift does it

Lift applies this rule from your own log and shows its working. Each exercise in a routine has a number of sets, one rep target, a rest time and a weight step. Lift uses a single rep target, not a range. A new exercise starts at 3 sets of 10, and you can change every one of those numbers when you edit the routine.

Before each exercise, Lift suggests a weight from the last session in which you did it:

  • Every set at the same weight, all at or above the target: it adds one step. Sessions 1 and 4 in the table.
  • Same weight, but a set below the target: it holds. Sessions 2, 3, 5 and 6.
  • The same weight three sessions running without clearing the target: it suggests about 10% less, rounded to your step. Session 7, where it suggests 40 kg.
  • Fewer sets done than planned: it holds until you finish them all. A skipped set is recorded as skipped and is never treated as a failed one, and if you skipped the whole exercise last time, Lift works from the session before.
  • The first time you do an exercise: there is no suggestion. You choose the weight, and Lift works from that next time.

There are a few more rules for sessions where the weight changed between sets. Tap Why on any suggestion to see the sets it used and the rule it applied.

The step starts from the type of equipment, for example 2.5 kg for machines, cables and barbells and 2 kg for dumbbells. Weight stacks differ, so set Step for each exercise in the routine editor to match yours. Bodyweight exercises start with no step, and Lift then holds the weight and says so. If you change an exercise's rep target, the next suggestion is rescaled to suit the new number of reps, and the normal rules carry on from there.

A suggestion never locks you in. Type a different weight and Lift works from what you actually did. Lift works in kilograms only.
