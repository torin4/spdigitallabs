How to check a house plan before you commit to it

14 November 2026 · 5 min read

Web app

Hanjo

A house planner for people building in Japan. Draw on the 910 mm module, see each room in jo and tsubo, then look around the house in 3D.

See Hanjo

A floor plan can look fine on paper and still be awkward to live in. Changes are cheap while the house is a drawing and expensive once it's built, so it's worth an hour of checking how you'd actually move through it.

Walk the daily routes

Pick the trips you make every day and trace each one on the plan with a finger:

  • From the front door to the kitchen, carrying shopping.
  • From the washing machine to where clothes dry, and from there to where they're put away.
  • From each bedroom to the toilet at night.
  • From the front door to the living room, as a guest would walk it.

Count the doors and corners on each route, and notice what the route passes through. A laundry route that crosses the living room, or a toilet that opens straight off the dining area, is easy to miss on a plan and hard to ignore in a house.

Put real furniture in

An empty room always looks big enough. Draw the furniture you own, or plan to buy, at its real size: the bed, the sofa, the dining table with its chairs pulled out, the fridge with its door open.

Then look at what's left. A common rule of thumb is that one person needs about 600 mm to walk past something, and more on a route two people use at once. Check the gap between the bed and the wall, behind the dining chairs, and between the kitchen counter and whatever faces it.

Check every door

A hinged door sweeps a quarter circle as wide as the door itself. For each one, ask:

  • Does it hit furniture, or another door, as it opens?
  • Does it open against a wall, or into the middle of the room?
  • Can you stand clear of it while you open it, especially in a toilet or a small washroom?

Sliding doors avoid the sweep, but they need a stretch of clear wall to slide along, and nothing can be fixed to that part of the wall.

See where the sun falls

The sun's height at noon depends on the season and on how far north you are. At noon it stands at 90° minus your latitude, plus or minus 23.4° for the tilt of the earth. At 35° north, about the latitude of Tokyo, Nagoya and Osaka, the winter figure is:

90° − 35° − 23.4° = 31.6°

That difference is what eaves are for. The high summer sun is blocked by the overhang above a south window, and the low winter sun comes in under it and reaches deep into the room. So look at each main room at three times of day in both summer and winter: where the morning sun lands, whether the living room gets winter sun at all, and whether a west window will make a room hot on summer afternoons.

Remember what is outside the plan. A neighbour's two-storey house to the south can shade your ground floor all winter, whatever your own drawing shows.

Doing it in Hanjo

Hanjo draws the 3D house from your plan, so these checks don't need a separate model. All of this is in the free part of the app. You need an account to use it.

Furniture and doors

The Library tool has furniture at real sizes: beds, sofas, dining tables, kitchens, unit baths, appliances. Pick an item and click the plan to place it. It snaps to a nearby wall, and holding Alt places it freely.

The Checks tab then lists what Hanjo finds, and clicking a line selects the thing on the plan. It reports "Furniture blocks a door" when something stands in the swing of a hinged door, or when less than 600 mm of clear way is left through a doorway. Rugs and other low things don't count. It also flags a room whose Use is Corridor when its clear width is under 750 mm, and stairs that are narrower than 750 mm, have risers over 230 mm or treads under 150 mm, which are Japan's minimums for stairs in a house. Those stair figures are legal minimums and make a steep stair. Most people want a gentler one.

3D and the walk-through

Switch from Plan to 3D at the top. Hide floors above cuts the house open at the floor you're on so you can look down into the rooms. Hide near walls removes whichever walls face you as you turn the house, Half-height inside walls cuts the partitions down, and Hide furniture clears the rooms.

Walk through puts you inside at an eye height of 1.5 m. Move with W, A, S and D or the arrow keys, drag to look around, and press Esc to leave. Walls stop you and doorways let you through, so a route that is awkward on the plan is awkward here too. You walk one floor at a time, and a small map shows where you are and lets you jump to another spot. A door can be shown open or closed in 3D, so you can see what an open door gets in the way of.

Sunlight

In 3D, the Time of day slider runs from 4:00 to 22:00 in half-hour steps, and Season switches between 21 June, the equinoxes and 21 December. Hanjo works out the sun's position from the Latitude and North settings of your site and shows it, for example "Sun 32° up, bearing 180°". The house casts shadows on itself and on the ground, and lamps come on after sunset.

The latitude starts at 35.5° north, so change it if you're building somewhere else, and set North to match your plot. The time is solar time, where noon is the moment the sun is due south, so it can differ a little from the clock.

What Hanjo doesn't check

  • Your neighbours. The 3D view shows your house and your plot. Buildings, trees and slopes outside it aren't modelled, so their shadows aren't either.
  • The routes themselves. Hanjo tells you when a door is blocked or a corridor is narrow. Whether the laundry route makes sense is still your call.
  • Structure. Nothing in Hanjo checks whether the house will stand up, and its regulation checks are simplified.

Hanjo is a planning tool, not an architect. Have a licensed architect (建築士) or builder check any design before you rely on it. The easiest way to get that check is to show them the plan: How to share a house plan with your builder and keep track of versions covers how.
